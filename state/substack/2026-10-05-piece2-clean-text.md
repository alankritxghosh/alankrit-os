# Why does a generalist with agents sometimes beat a specialist without them?

Last time I went through the research on generalists. Breadth predicts who starts things. It doesn't reliably predict who does well.

Which leaves the question I actually care about. What changes once you have agents?

Some context on why I'm asking. I'm a curious builder with a degree in finance, and I work inside tech and marketing. I love art, beautiful design, poetry and photos, and I try to bring all of it into my work.

With agents I can do a lot of things that I think a specialist would take longer on, or couldn't do at all. That's my view. I wanted to see what the research says about it.

I found a study that comes close. Researchers at Harvard Business School ran it with IG Group, a derivatives trading firm. 78 people wrote investing articles for the company's website with AI help. 12 were the web analysts who write those articles for a living. 26 were marketing specialists. 40 were technology specialists, developers and data scientists.

The marketing group got close to the analysts. Their articles averaged 3.92 out of 5 against 3.96 for the analysts. The technology group averaged 3.42, half a point lower, about 13% below the analysts. The paper is called The GenAI Wall Effect, and HBS describes the idea as "knowledge distance". The further you sit from the task, the less AI helps you.

So a generalist, as I read it, is someone who sits close to a lot of tasks. Close to them, short of expert. And being close is what lets you tell whether what the model gave you is any good. That's the only version of my claim I can back up.

For me, that was Icarus.

I'm a finance student, not an engineer. From idea to launch on Product Hunt, Icarus took me 60 days. Engineering, marketing and distribution, research, costing it properly, and the design language, where I studied what Wispr Flow had done and how simple they kept it. Each of those was a lane next to the one I knew.

When I was engineering it, I looked at marketing and distribution the way a builder would. I kept asking what an agent could do instead of me, because a lot of those tasks looked trivial. That got me to a marketing system I could use much earlier than I otherwise would have.

Finance went the same way. I understand economics, so I could build the whole product without spending money. I could see every problem through more than one lens.

Now the part that doesn't go my way.

BCG and a team of academics ran a study with 758 consultants using GPT-4. On 18 tasks inside what the model could do, consultants with AI completed 12.2% more tasks on average, finished 25.1% faster, and produced results rated more than 40% higher in quality. Then the researchers picked a task that sat outside what the model could do. The consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get a correct answer than the ones without it.

That bit worries me, because it describes exactly where a generalist is exposed. You're close enough to feel sure and not close enough to catch the mistake.

Procter & Gamble ran a field experiment with 776 professionals. People working alone with AI performed at a level comparable to teams of two without it. R&D people, who lean technical, and commercial people, who lean commercial, both came up with more balanced ideas once they had AI. To me that says AI makes it cheaper to work in the lane next door. It says nothing about winning there.

So, sometimes. Agents widen the set of things you can do well, and that set has an edge. The edge is wherever your judgment runs out. A specialist without agents is slower but usually knows where theirs is. A generalist with agents is fast and can walk straight past it.

What I do about it: I work relentlessly. I run into complex problems, build things, watch them break, and work out a way around. Breaking is how I find the edge.

The one that stuck with me was Icarus's retrieval. It started on keyword searches, which made it just another sophisticated RAG pipeline, and I didn't want that. Moving it to semantic retrieval took one weekend, Saturday and Sunday, and it broke the whole way through. The worst of it was a timeout bug in the cloud where the brain was hosted. That one ran for 30 hours. My agents and I fixed it, and we understood why it happened.

Engineering isn't my lane. But I knew what I wanted the system to be, and I could tell when it wasn't that. That weekend is where I found out how far my edge goes.

How do you find your edge before an agent finds it for you?
