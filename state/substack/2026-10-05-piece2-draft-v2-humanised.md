Last time I went through the research on generalists. Breadth predicts who starts things. It doesn't reliably predict who does well.

Which leaves the question I actually care about. What changes once you have agents?

Some context on why I'm asking. I'm a curious builder with a degree in finance, and I work inside tech and marketing. I love art, beautiful design, poetry and photos, and I try to bring all of it into my work.

With agents I can do a lot of things that I think a specialist would take longer on, or couldn't do at all. That's my view. I wanted to see what the research says about it.

I found a study that comes close. Researchers from Stanford and Harvard Business School ran it at IG Group, a UK fintech. [VERIFY authors and title] 78 people wrote web articles with AI help. [VERIFY] Some were the experts who write those articles for a living. Some were marketing staff from the same department who had never written articles but knew roughly what the writers did. Some were developers and data scientists with no writing or marketing background. [VERIFY]

The marketing group got close to the experts. The developers didn't, and trailed the marketing and web people by 13% on clarity and competence. [VERIFY exact measure] The researchers call this expertise distance. The further you sit from the task, the less AI helps you. [VERIFY wording]

So a generalist, as I read it, is someone who sits close to a lot of tasks. Close to them, short of expert. And being close is what lets you tell whether what the model gave you is any good. That's the only version of my claim I can back up.

For me, that was Icarus.

I'm a finance student, not an engineer. I took Icarus from idea to a live product. Engineering, marketing and distribution, research, costing it properly, and the design language, where I studied what Wispr Flow had done and how simple they kept it. Each of those was a lane next to the one I knew.

When I was engineering it, I looked at marketing and distribution the way a builder would. I kept asking what an agent could do instead of me, because a lot of those tasks looked trivial. That got me to a marketing system I could use much earlier than I otherwise would have.

Finance went the same way. I understand economics, so I could build the whole product without spending money. [CHECK: is this literally true? If you paid for anything, say what.] I had a usable product in under 50 days. [CHECK: your 1 October piece says under 60. Use one number in both.] I could see every problem through more than one lens.

Now the part that doesn't go my way.

BCG ran a study with 758 consultants using GPT-4. [VERIFY] On tasks inside what the model could do, consultants with AI finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at more than 40% higher quality. [VERIFY] Then the researchers picked a task they knew sat outside what the model could do. The consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer than the ones without it. [VERIFY]

That bit worries me, because it describes exactly where a generalist is exposed. You're close enough to feel sure and not close enough to catch the mistake.

Procter & Gamble ran a field experiment with 776 professionals. [VERIFY] One person with AI did about as well as a team of two without it. [VERIFY wording] R&D people, who lean technical, and commercial people, who lean commercial, both came up with more balanced ideas once they had AI. [VERIFY] To me that says AI makes it cheaper to work in the lane next door. It says nothing about winning there.

So, sometimes. Agents widen the set of things you can do well, and that set has an edge. The edge is wherever your judgment runs out. A specialist without agents is slower but usually knows where theirs is. A generalist with agents is fast and can walk straight past it.

What I do about it: I work relentlessly. I run into complex problems, build things, watch them break, and work out a way around. Breaking is how I find the edge. [CHECK: keep only if true.]

The one that stuck with me was Icarus's retrieval. At first it ran on keyword searches, which made it just another sophisticated RAG pipeline, and I didn't want that. I kept moving toward a semantic retrieval system and things kept breaking. Then came timeouts in the cloud where the brain was hosted. That bug lasted 30 hours. My agents and I fixed it, and we understood why it happened. [CHECK: you also said "a few days of headaches". Add a line if the wider retrieval work took days.]
